Where this data already comes from

Nothing here is new data. A camera crew has already been down these pipes.

A German sewer utility commissions an inspection. A crew drives a CCTV crawler through a pipe section, or lowers a camera into a manhole, and logs what it sees at each station — the distance along the pipe. The result is delivered as a DWA-M 150 exchange file, the standard German format for passing sewer inspection data between contractor and municipality.

That file nests three levels: the asset (HG pipe, KG node), the inspection event that visited it (HI, KI), and each individual observation recorded along the way (HZ, KZ). A sibling project, m150-onto, converts that XML into a graph — every pipe section, manhole, inspection and condition report becomes an addressable thing rather than a row.

This layer already worked. Every single observation shown on this page comes from it. But converting XML into a graph does not make the graph mean anything — the damage codes arrive as literal strings, exactly as opaque as they were in the file.

The problem: the meaning lives in a PDF

DIN EN 13508-2 defines codes, all present in SDCO.rdf. This one small dataset uses of them. What each code means is written down — in the standard, on paper — and carried in the heads of the inspectors who use it daily. It is nowhere in the data.

Here is what that costs you. These two codes describe the same physical defect: a fissure, a crack in the wall.

They share no characters. No string match, no wildcard, no LIKE 'BA%' will ever group them. To ask “show me every fissure in my network” you must already know the standard well enough to enumerate the codes by hand — and re-check your list every time the standard is revised.

What the codes are actually saying

The code system is not arbitrary. Its first letter says where the observation was made; its second says what kind of thing was observed. Laid out on those two axes, the whole scheme becomes readable at a glance — every code the standard defines lands in a cell, whether or not this sample dataset happens to use it. Solid chips were observed in this dataset; dashed chips exist in the standard but not here.

Fabric — structural damage Operation — blockage, roots, infiltration Inventory — features, not damage Other — remarks, photos, termination dashed — not in this sample

And the same codes, as meanings

SDCO restates the standard as a hierarchy of concepts. A crack is a kind of fissure; a fissure is a kind of structural defect; a structural defect is a kind of observation. Ask about any level and you get everything beneath it, automatically.

Most of this tree is greyed out or hidden, because this is a 96-observation sample of a standard built for entire networks. That is honest: the taxonomy covers the standard, not just the example.

The bridge is one sentence

Connecting the operator's existing data to all of that meaning takes a single statement, repeated once per code:

:BAB  ≡  hasConditionCode value "BAB"

Read it aloud: anything whose condition code is “BAB” is a BAB — and a BAB, the ontology already knows, is a fissure in a pipe. That is the whole join. From there the hierarchy does the rest without being told anything further.

It attaches to the data through exactly three properties the M150 file already contains: hasConditionCode, hasCharacterization1 and hasCharacterization2. No re-entry, no migration, no new field for anyone to fill in. The inspection reports stay exactly as they were written.

What that buys, in three words

These were the project's own research questions. They are the right three, and everything above is the evidence.

Interoperability

The codes stop being local strings and become shared concepts. Two utilities, two software vendors, one CCTV contractor and an AI defect detector can all mean the same thing by “fissure” — because they point at the same definition, derived from the standard rather than from anyone's database schema.

Query complexity

“Every fissure in the network” goes from a hand-maintained list of codes you must know the standard to write, to naming the concept once. The list cannot drift out of date, because there is no list.

Inference

Nobody wrote down that this manhole crack runs vertically. A reasoner derived it from the code and its characterization, then wrote it back as a fact you can query. Meaning that was implicit in the standard becomes data.

What this doesn't do yet

This is a working prototype, and it is worth being precise about its edges.

  • No domain-expert validation. The taxonomy is derived from the standard by reading it carefully. No practising sewer inspector has reviewed it yet. That review is the single most valuable thing that could happen to it next.
  • The reasoning needs a materialization step. The classification uses existential restrictions (someValuesFrom), which let a reasoner prove that this report is a vertical crack fissure, but never leave behind a plain triple saying so. A separate pass walks the proven types back through the restrictions and writes the literal triples out — that is where the numbers on this page come from, and it has to be re-run when the data changes.
  • The ontology itself is barely annotated. Across classes there is exactly one human-readable label and a few dozen comments. Every plain-English gloss on this page was written by hand for this page. The ontology is precise; it is not yet self-explanatory.
  • It is one worked example. 96 observations across 4 pipe sections and 5 manholes, from the standard's own textbook dataset. It demonstrates the mechanism; it does not prove it at network scale.

Where it goes

SDCO exists to serve KaSyTwin — semi-automated digital twins of sewer networks built from multi-sensor robotic platforms, with AI detecting damage from the footage in real time. When a crawler's neural network flags a crack, it needs somewhere to put that finding that isn't a three-letter string. This is the target schema for that: the same vocabulary, whether the observation came from an inspector's eye in 2006 or a model's output tomorrow. From there, condition data that carries its own meaning is what predictive maintenance — which pipe fails next, and when — actually needs underneath it.
